1. Executive Summary
Paloma's  concern  is  fair  and  worth  taking  seriously.  The  honest  answer  is  nuanced:  Brazilian
sugarcane  has  a  genuine  sustainability  story  (decoupled  from  Amazon  deforestation,  net  carbon
removals, falling water intensity, pre-harvest burning phase-out) — but it also has real environmental
costs (monoculture pressure, agrochemicals, soil compaction, Cerrado expansion risk, and indirect
land-use effects). A responsible buyer like TrueTech Inc. can and should select mills that are certified
(RenovaBio / Bonsucro) and verifiably not driving native-vegetation conversion.
Mission tie-in: The DAO's mission is restoring 10,000 hectares of Amazon rainforest. Any
sugar sourcing decision should favor suppliers whose operations are deforestation-free,
certified, and land-positive — so our trade volume reinforces, rather than contradicts, the
mission. 
2. Land-Use Facts: What the Data Actually Shows
2.1 Sugarcane expansion is large but overwhelmingly on existing farmland
Sugarcane expanded +6.06 million hectares (Mha) across Brazil's center-south and north
between 2000 and 2020, reaching 8.45 Mha in 2020 (MDPI Land, 2023).
98.4% of that expansion was over existing agricultural areas — mostly poor-quality
pasture (55%), cropland (15%), and mosaic land (14%).
Net result 2000–2020: a net carbon removal of ~9.8 Tg CO2/yr, driven by converting
degraded pasture to cane plus the shift from burned to unburned harvest.
Why? Sugarcane is highly productive per hectare — it produces more energy and sugar
per unit of land than most alternatives, so it relieves land pressure rather than adding to it
when sited on degraded pasture.
2.2 The Amazon: legally protected, and industry has stayed out
A 2009 federal decree bans sugarcane production in the Amazon biome; a 2018 senate
attempt to lift the ban was defeated after opposition from UNICA (the mills' own union),
Greenpeace, and five former environment ministers.
Amazonian states produced <1% of Brazil's cane in 2018/19.
RenovaBio requires zero deforestation in sugarcane areas post-2018 — a hard national
standard for certified producers.

2.3 The real risk zones: Cerrado & indirect effects
The Cerrado (savanna) biome is the actual expansion frontier — pressure there is real, and
sugarcane joins soy and pasture as drivers of native-vegetation conversion.
Indirect land-use change (ILUC): a 2017 study (Jusys) found sugarcane indirectly
contributed to deforestation 2002–2012 by displacing pasture/crops into forest frontiers.
CEPAL/USP modeling shows each extra sugarcane hectare was associated with a 0.47 ha
fall in pasture — which must go somewhere.
Para note: ESALQ estimates Para could theoretically host up to 9 Mha of cane — but CONAB
puts actual Para harvest at ~736,000 tons, a rounding error nationally. A "Para mill" would be
unusual and worth extra due diligence specifically because the region is not a traditional cane
belt.

4. Certification & Policy: The Buyer's Toolkit
RenovaBio (ANP, 2017– ): national biofuel certification; carbon-intensity scoring via
RenovaCalc; zero-deforestation rule; generates tradable CBIO decarbonization credits. Mills
self-report and are audited by accredited inspection bodies.
Bonsucro: global gold-standard sustainability certification for sugarcane — covers GHG,
water, biodiversity, labor, living wage. Certified producers show -31% farm-level and -20%

Fuel of the Future Law (2024/25): raises the ethanol blend ceiling, expanding demand —

reputational and regulatory risk — and increasingly locked out of premium markets. 
5. What This Means for the 50,000 t/Month Deal
Ask for certification up front. Shortlist mills with RenovaBio (mandatory for ethanol co-
producers) and Bonsucro where possible.
Add a sustainability clause to the LOI/contract: no native-vegetation conversion on supply
areas; compliance with the 2009 Amazon biome decree; green (unburned) harvest preferred.
Due diligence on any "Para mill" — Para is outside the traditional cane belt and inside the
Amazon biome boundary; verify land tenure, CAR registration, and any deforestation linkage
before contracting. This connects directly to Milena's point about visiting and certifying
facilities.
Position it as a feature: Brazilian cane sugar (esp. certified, low-ILUC) is among the most
land-efficient sweeteners globally — ~9.8 Tg CO2/yr net removals across the sector. TrueTech
can present this to Chinese buyers as a defensible, ESG-clean supply chain.
Volumetric context: 50,000 t/month ≈ 600,000 t/yr — roughly 0.7% of Brazil's ~85 Mt sugar
output. One large certified mill (e.g. Raizen, Tereos, Sao Martinho) can supply it without any
land-use change at the margin; it is not a driver of new conversion.
